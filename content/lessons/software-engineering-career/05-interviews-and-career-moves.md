Changing jobs is a skill of its own, separate from being a good engineer. Plenty of strong engineers stall at the résumé, freeze in a coding interview, or accept the first offer. Each step has a few habits that make a big difference.

## A résumé that gets read

Recruiters often spend under a minute on a résumé before deciding. Make that minute count.

- **Lead with results, not duties.** "Responsible for the checkout page" says what you were assigned. "Rebuilt checkout, cutting load time from 4s to 1.2s and abandoned carts by 9%" says what you achieved.
- **Use numbers** where you have them: speed, users, money, time saved, error rates. Rough ones are fine if they're honest.
- **Name the tools** you actually used, so the résumé matches the job ad's keywords.
- **Keep it to one page,** two at most for long careers. Newest work first.
- **Tailor it.** Move the most relevant experience to the top for each role you apply to.

A referral from someone at the company often beats any résumé. Tell former colleagues you're looking.

## Coding interviews

Most coding interviews test how you solve problems with someone watching, more than whether you know the perfect trick.

1. **Repeat the problem back** and ask about the edges: empty input, duplicates, very large input.
2. **Talk through a simple solution first,** even a slow one. Then improve it. A working slow solution beats an unfinished clever one.
3. **Think out loud.** The interviewer can only give you credit, and hints, for reasoning they can hear.
4. **Test your code** by walking through a small example by hand, including an edge case.
5. **Know your complexity.** Be ready to say how time and memory grow with input size.

Practice under realistic conditions: a timer, talking out loud, and a plain editor. Fifty problems done that way teach more than two hundred done silently.

## System design and behavioral interviews

For **system design**, follow the order from the system design lesson: requirements, a simple design, then trade-offs as the numbers grow. Interviewers want to see you reason, not recite.

For **behavioral** questions ("Tell me about a disagreement with a teammate"), prepare five or six real stories and tell them with **STAR**: the Situation, the Task, the Action *you* took, and the Result. Include what you learned. One story can answer several questions.

## Negotiating an offer

Most offers have room to move, and asking politely doesn't make companies take them back.

- **Know your number** before the call. Check salary data for the role, level and city (Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, local surveys).
- **Avoid naming a number first** if you can. "I'd like to learn more about the role first. What range did you have in mind?" works.
- **Get the offer in writing,** then take a day or two. It's normal.
- **Negotiate the whole package:** base pay, signing bonus, equity, start date, remote days, title, learning budget. If base pay is fixed, other parts often aren't.
- **Use real leverage honestly.** A competing offer is the strongest lever. Never invent one.
- **Be warm and specific.** "I'm excited about this role. With my experience in X, I was hoping for a base closer to Y. Is there flexibility?"

## Deciding to move

Money matters, but so do what you'll learn, who you'll learn it from, and whether the team ships. Ask in interviews how they review code, how they plan work, and what happened after their last outage. The answers tell you a lot about the job.

## Problem

You've received an offer of $120,000 base. Salary data for the role in your city suggests $125,000 to $140,000, and you have another interview process still in progress. Write what you'd say on the call with the recruiter.

## Solution

One good version:

> "Thank you, I'm really excited about the team and the work. I've looked at what similar roles pay here, and with my experience leading the payments migration, I was hoping for something closer to $135,000. I'm also in the final stages with another company, so I'd love to see if we can get there. Is there flexibility on the base, or on a signing bonus?"

It thanks them and shows enthusiasm, gives a specific number backed by data and experience, mentions the other process honestly without bluffing about an offer you don't have, and opens a second option if the base is fixed. Then stop talking and let them answer.
