"AI" gets stuck on everything from washing machines to chatbots. So what does it actually mean?

## A working definition

**Artificial intelligence** is software that does tasks we used to think needed human judgment: recognizing a face, understanding a sentence, choosing a good chess move, or writing a paragraph.

That definition moves over time. A calculator once looked like magic, and so did a computer that could beat a chess champion. Once a trick becomes ordinary, people tend to stop calling it AI.

## Two ways to make a program smart

**1. Write the rules by hand.** A programmer spells out every step: "if the email contains *you have won*, mark it as spam." This works when the rules are clear, but it breaks fast. Spammers change their wording, and nobody can write rules for "what a cat looks like."

**2. Let the program learn from examples.** Instead of rules, you show the program thousands of emails already marked "spam" or "not spam," and it works out the patterns itself. This is called **machine learning**, and it's behind nearly all the AI in the news today.

## Narrow, not general

Every AI you can use today is **narrow**: it's good at the kind of task it was built and trained for. A photo app that finds your dog can't plan a trip. Chatbots feel broader because language covers so many subjects, but they still have clear limits, which later lessons cover.

**General AI**, a system that can learn and do almost any mental task as well as a person, is a goal some labs are working toward. Experts disagree about how close it is.

## Where you already meet it

- Your phone grouping photos by the people in them
- Maps predicting traffic and arrival times
- Streaming services suggesting what to watch next
- Your bank flagging a strange card payment
- Voice assistants turning speech into text
- Chatbots answering questions and drafting writing

All of these learned their skills from examples rather than from hand-written rules. The next lesson shows how that learning works.

## Which of these is an example of narrow AI?

- A machine that can do any job a person can
- [x] A photo app that recognizes faces
- A robot that learns every skill on its own

Narrow AI is good at the kind of task it was trained for. A face-finding photo app is a classic example. A machine that can do any job would be general AI, which doesn't exist yet.

## Why is "if the email says *you have won*, it's spam" a weak way to catch spam?

Hand-written rules are easy to get around. A learning system can keep picking up new patterns from fresh examples.

## Problem

Think of three apps or devices you used this week. For each one, decide whether it probably uses AI, and whether that AI would be easier to build with hand-written rules or by learning from examples.

## Solution

There's no single right answer, but here's how to reason about it. Ask: *could someone write down exact rules for this?*

- A **calculator** or **alarm clock**: clear rules, no AI needed.
- A **keyboard that suggests the next word**: nobody can list every phrase people type, so it learns from lots of text.
- A **map's arrival time**: it learns from past trips how traffic behaves at different times.

If the task involves messy, real-world input like photos, speech, or everyday language, it almost always uses learning from examples.
